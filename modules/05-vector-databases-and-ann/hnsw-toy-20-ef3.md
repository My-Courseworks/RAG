# HNSW toy, 20 points, `M = 2`, `ef_construction = 3`

The README's 8-point toy inserted sorted values, so every walk started next to the new point, the
pool of candidates never filled, and `ef_construction` had nothing to do. This document answers
"what if 20 points and `ef_construction = 3`?" by making the dial bite: 20 distinct integers in
[1, 40] inserted in a shuffled order, so the walk has to travel and the 3-slot pool overflows. The
graph below is what hnswlib 0.8.0 builds (`l2`, `dim=1`, `M=2`, `ef_construction=3`, one thread),
read back from its pickled state; the narration comes from a Python re-implementation of
hnswlib's `addPoint` that reproduces hnswlib's neighbour lists, stored order included, on every
layer. The same points, order and levels were then built with `ef_construction = 200` and the
two graphs diffed. Headline: the graphs differ on layer 0 only, the whole difference traces to
one admission decision at insert 7, and that decision leaves F(18) without its true nearest
neighbour and makes every query whose answer is F wrong at query time, whatever `ef` is.

How to read the step blocks:

- The walk starts at the entry point on the top layer. On layers above the new node's top it is
  greedy with a pool of 1: hop along the layer while a neighbour is nearer, then slide down the
  pole to the same node on the layer below.
- On each layer from the node's top down to 0, `searchBaseLayer` keeps a pool of the
  `ef_construction = 3` nearest nodes seen so far. It pops the nearest unexpanded candidate,
  looks at its neighbour list, and admits a neighbour only if the pool has room or the neighbour
  is strictly nearer than the pool's worst (`lowerBound > dist`). Admitting into a full pool
  evicts the worst. It stops when the nearest unexpanded candidate is farther than the worst in
  the full pool (`-curr.first > lowerBound && size == ef`), or when nothing is left to expand.
- From that pool `getNeighborsByHeuristic2` keeps at most `M = 2`: nearest first, then a
  candidate only if it is nearer to the new point than to every neighbour already kept; a
  candidate that fails is "behind" one. With fewer than `M` candidates it keeps all.
- `mutuallyConnectNewElement` writes the edge into both lists. If the neighbour's list is at
  its cap (`Mmax = 2` on layers ≥ 1, `Mmax0 = 4` on layer 0) the same heuristic is rerun over
  the neighbour's list plus the new node, with distances measured from the neighbour, keeping at
  most the cap. Nodes it drops still list the neighbour: that edge becomes one-way. The nearest
  kept node is where the walk starts on the layer below.
- Distances are shown as `|a − b|`; hnswlib compares squared L2, which orders 1-D points the
  same way, ties included. Ties are frequent (20 integers in [1, 40]) and hnswlib breaks them by
  whatever order its `std::priority_queue` happens to hold, so the simulation models libc++'s
  heap exactly rather than sorting; the verification section reports the match.

### Where `ef_construction = 3` mattered

- **One admission decision did all the damage: insert 7, G(10).** Line so far
  `C2 · E4 · A8 · G10 · F18 · B24 · D33`. The walk reached E(4) on layer 0 and the pool filled
  as `[A2 E6 C8]`. Expanding A offered F(18) at d=8. Admission requires strictly nearer than the
  worst, and F ties the worst, C(8), so F was refused. F is the nearest point on G's right, and
  the selection rule would have kept it (d(F, A) = 10 > d(F, G) = 8, so not behind A). With
  `ef_construction = 200` the pool holds everything reachable and G gets two links, G–A and G–F.
- **The same step cut the last left-to-right links.** A's layer-0 list `{B, C, E, F}` hit its
  cap of 4 when G arrived and was pruned to `{E, G}`: A–B (from insert 2) and A–F (from insert 6)
  were the only edges from a node ≤ 15 to a node ≥ 18, and both went. F→A and B→A survive one-way.
  Under `ef=200` the G–F edge written in that very step keeps the two halves of the line joined
  on layer 0; under `ef=3` nothing does, and nothing later repairs it.
- **Two later walks that start on the left never see F.** Insert 10, J(14): pool `[G4 A6 E10]`,
  early stop, J–G only; with `ef=200` J keeps G(4) and F(4) and writes J–F. Insert 18, R(15):
  pool `[J1 L3 G5]`, R–J only; with `ef=200` R keeps J(1) and F(3) and writes R–F. Those are the
  two mutual edges on the `ef=200` side of the diff (`J→F, F→J, R→F, F→R`).
- **The `ef=3`-only side of the diff is second order.** `G→A, G→J, F→A, F→B` exist only under
  `ef=3` because the lists were never pruned there: under `ef=200`, F's list had collected G and J
  and reached its cap at insert 11 (K), where it was pruned to `{K, J}`; G's list had collected F
  and reached its cap at insert 19 (S), where it was pruned to `{L, S}`. Under `ef=3` neither list
  ever reached 4, so the stale one-way edges stayed.
- **Upper layers are identical in both builds**, although the pool also filled there (insert 14,
  N: C refused on layer 1; insert 16, P: E refused on layer 1). Everything refused or evicted on
  layers ≥ 1 was on the same side of the new point as, and farther than, a kept neighbour, so the
  selection rule would have rejected it anyway.
- **Scoreboard.** 32 pool-full events (12 evictions, 20 refusals) and 7 early stops in the `ef=3`
  build. Only three events dropped a node that was alone on its side of the new point, which is
  the only situation in which 1-D's selection rule could have kept it: B(24) and F(18) at insert
  7, and A(8) at insert 11. B was behind F, and at insert 11 F(18) entered the pool later and
  covered A, so only the refusal of F at insert 7 changed an edge. Every other event evicted or
  refused a node the heuristic would have rejected: in one dimension a pool of 3 nearer points
  always has at least two on one side, so a node admitted fourth is behind one of them unless all
  three sit on the other side.
- **The miss is permanent and visible at query time.** F(18)'s true nearest is R(15), and F lists
  `{A, B, K, T}`; R lists `{J}`. With query `ef = 200` the `ef=3` index answers 3 of 40 `k=1`
  queries wrong (17.3, 18.3, 19.3: every query whose answer is F) and 4 of 40 `k=2` queries; the

  because L is nearer (7.3 vs 7.7), lands on the left half of layer 0, and returns R(15) at d=4.3
  while F(18) at d=1.3 and T(21) at d=1.7 are unreachable from there. Raising `ef` at query time

### Takeaways

- The pool is per layer and starts over from one node on each layer: `ef_construction` is the
  width of the search that *finds* candidates, `M` is how many of them the new node *keeps*.
  Width matters only when the pool overflows or the stop rule fires before the walk has seen
  the new point's true neighbourhood.
- Inserting sorted values hides the dial: every walk starts beside the new point, the pool never
  fills, and `ef_construction` is a no-op. Shuffle the order and the walk has to travel; 20
  points and a pool of 3 produced 32 pool-full events and 7 early stops.
- In one dimension almost every overflow is harmless, because the selection rule keeps at most
  one neighbour per side and a pool of 3 nearer points covers at least one side. The exceptions
  are nodes alone on their side, and a single refused one (F at insert 7) split layer 0 for good.
- Pruning is one-sided in hnswlib: the pruned node drops the edge, the other end keeps it. The
  README's "links are bidirectional" holds when a list is first written; 8 prunes here left 8
  one-way edges, and D's layer-1 list went `{C, E}` → `{L}` → `{L, N}` → `{P}` across two prunes.
  An edge can also vanish from both ends: E–D on layer 1 was dropped by D's prune and by E's
  prune in the same insert (12, L).
- In 1,536 dimensions "another direction" is cheap, so the heuristic keeps all `M` links and a
  node refused from the pool is rarely covered by a nearer one on the same side. The overflow
  events that were harmless here become missing edges at scale, which is why engines default
  `ef_construction` to 100–200 against `M = 16`.
- `ef` at query time is the same `searchBaseLayer` on layer 0 with the same stop rule; it widens
  the pool but walks the same edges. A graph built too narrow is repaired by a rebuild, not by a
  bigger `ef`.
